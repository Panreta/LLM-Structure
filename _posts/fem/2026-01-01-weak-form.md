Multiply the equation by a **test function** $$v$$ with $$v(0) = v(1) = 0$$, integrate, and move one derivative onto $$v$$ by integration by parts:

$$\int_0^1 -u'' v \, dx = \bigl[-u' v\bigr]_0^1 + \int_0^1 u' v' \, dx = \int_0^1 u' v' \, dx$$

The boundary term vanishes because $$v$$ is zero at the ends. Now only *first* derivatives appear, and only inside integrals. The natural space is

$$H^1_0(0, 1) = \{ v : v, v' \in L^2(0,1),\ v(0) = v(1) = 0 \}$$

with the norm $$\lVert v \rVert = \lVert v' \rVert_{L^2}$$, which is a norm thanks to the zero boundary values. It contains functions with kinks, which is exactly what finite elements will be.

Every classical solution is a weak solution. Conversely, a weak solution that happens to be twice differentiable is a classical one. The weak problem is a genuine extension, not a different problem.

**The figure** checks the identity for a bump-shaped test function $$v$$ (dashed). The integrands $$u'v'$$ (blue) and $$fv$$ (brown) are different curves, but their areas agree. Move and resize the bump: they agree for every $$v$$, which is what "weak solution" means.
