A computer cannot search all of $$H^1_0$$. **Galerkin's method:** choose a finite-dimensional subspace $$V_h \subset V$$ and ask the weak equation only for test functions in it:

$$\text{find } u_h \in V_h: \quad a(u_h, v_h) = \ell(v_h) \quad \text{for all } v_h \in V_h$$

Coercivity holds on any subspace, so Lax–Milgram applies again: $$u_h$$ exists and is unique.

**Galerkin orthogonality.** Subtracting the two equations gives $$a(u - u_h, v_h) = 0$$ for all $$v_h \in V_h$$: the error is orthogonal to the subspace in the sense of $$a$$.

**Céa's lemma.** For any $$v_h \in V_h$$,

$$\alpha \lVert u - u_h \rVert^2 \le a(u - u_h, u - u_h) = a(u - u_h, u - v_h) \le M \lVert u - u_h \rVert \lVert u - v_h \rVert$$

using orthogonality in the middle. Divide by $$\lVert u - u_h \rVert$$ to get the bound above: **Galerkin is as good as the best approximation from $$V_h$$, up to the constant $$M/\alpha$$.** The analysis of the method now reduces to a question of approximation theory: how well can functions in $$V_h$$ approximate $$u$$?

**The figure** is the symmetric case drawn in $$\mathbb{R}^2$$, with the energy inner product $$\langle x, y \rangle_a = x^\top A y$$. $$V_h$$ is the line. $$u_h$$ is the $$a$$-orthogonal projection of $$u$$: the dashed ellipse, a circle in the energy norm, touches the line exactly at $$u_h$$, so no other point of $$V_h$$ is closer in energy. The ordinary projection (gray ring) is worse in that norm. Drag $$u$$ and turn the line.
