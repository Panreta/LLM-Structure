Write $$u_h = \sum_j U_j \phi_j$$ and test with each hat $$v_h = \phi_i$$:

$$\sum_j a(\phi_j, \phi_i)\, U_j = \ell(\phi_i), \qquad i = 1, \dots, N-1$$

The weak problem has become a linear system. $$K$$ is the **stiffness matrix** and $$F$$ the **load vector**, names inherited from structural mechanics.

**Element by element.** Nobody computes $$K_{ij}$$ entry by entry. Loop over the elements instead. On one element of length $$h$$, the two hats that live there have slopes $$\pm 1/h$$, giving the local matrix

$$K^{(e)} = \frac1h \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

which is added into the rows and columns of the element's two nodes. Entries belonging to the fixed boundary nodes are dropped. Every interior node collects two blocks, so

$$K = \frac1h \operatorname{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)$$

**The figure** highlights one element and the four entries it touches. Three properties follow from the theory:

- **Sparse**, from local support: about $$3N$$ nonzeros instead of $$N^2$$.
- **Symmetric**, because $$a$$ is.
- **Positive definite**, because $$U^\top K U = a(u_h, u_h) \ge \alpha \lVert u_h \rVert^2 > 0$$ for $$U \ne 0$$: Lax–Milgram's coercivity, seen by the matrix.

The same loop, with triangles or tetrahedra and their own local matrices, assembles the 2-D and 3-D problems in every FEM code.
