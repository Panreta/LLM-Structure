Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) is momentum and RMSProp together:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_k &= \beta_1 \mathbf{m}_{k-1} + (1-\beta_1)\,\mathbf{g}_k \\ \mathbf{v}_k &= \beta_2 \mathbf{v}_{k-1} + (1-\beta_2)\,\mathbf{g}_k^2 \end{aligned}$$

The averages start at zero, so early on they are too small. Bias correction divides that out:

$$\hat{\mathbf{m}}_k = \frac{\mathbf{m}_k}{1 - \beta_1^k}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{v}}_k = \frac{\mathbf{v}_k}{1 - \beta_2^k}$$

The figure uses $$\beta_2 = 0.99$$; libraries default to 0.999, which suits runs of thousands of steps.

Compare with the dashed RMSProp path. On this clean valley Adam is not faster: its momentum carries it past the valley floor, and it curves back in late. Set $$\beta_1 = 0$$ and it reduces to RMSProp with $$\rho = \beta_2$$, plus bias correction.

Adam wins in practice because real gradients come from minibatches and are noisy. Averaging with $$\beta_1$$ cancels much of that noise, and the per-coordinate scale needs little tuning. It became the default optimizer for deep learning.
