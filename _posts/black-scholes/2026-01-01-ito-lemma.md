**Itô processes.** $$dX_t = \mu_t\, dt + \sigma_t\, dW_t$$ means

$$X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t \mu_s\, ds + \int_0^t \sigma_s\, dW_s$$

for adapted $$\mu, \sigma$$ with $$\int_0^T \lvert\mu_s\rvert\, ds < \infty$$ and $$\int_0^T \sigma_s^2\, ds < \infty$$ almost surely.

**Theorem (Itô, 1951).** If $$f(t, x)$$ is once continuously differentiable in $$t$$ and twice in $$x$$ ($$f \in C^{1,2}$$), then $$f(t, X_t)$$ is an Itô process with the differential above, that is

$$f(t, X_t) = f(0, X_0) + \int_0^t \bigl(f_t + \mu f_x + \tfrac12\sigma^2 f_{xx}\bigr)\, ds + \int_0^t \sigma f_x\, dW_s$$

**Why the extra term.** Expand to second order:

$$df = f_t\, dt + f_x\, dX + \tfrac12 f_{xx}\, (dX)^2 + \dots$$

With the multiplication table of quadratic variation, $$(dX)^2 = \sigma^2 (dW)^2 = \sigma^2 dt$$, which is first order and does not vanish. All higher terms are $$o(dt)$$. The proof makes this rigorous by Taylor's theorem on a partition and the $$L^2$$ convergence of $$\sum f_{xx}(\Delta W)^2$$ to $$\int f_{xx}\, dt$$.

**Example.** $$f(x) = e^x$$ with $$X = W$$ ($$\mu = 0$$, $$\sigma = 1$$):

$$d e^{W_t} = e^{W_t}\, dW_t + \tfrac12 e^{W_t}\, dt$$

**The figure** checks it on one path. The partition sums of the right-hand side (blue dashed) reproduce $$e^{W_t}$$ (black), with an error that shrinks as the partition is refined. The ordinary chain rule (brown) drops $$\tfrac12 e^{W}\, dt$$ and falls systematically below.
