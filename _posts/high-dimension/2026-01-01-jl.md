An almost-orthogonal matrix is almost an isometry, even when it maps to far fewer dimensions.

**Lemma.** Take $$N$$ points $$v_1, \dots, v_N \in \mathbb{R}^m$$ and a random $$Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$$ with entries $$q_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tfrac1n)$$. If $$n \ge 24 \log N / \varepsilon^2$$, then with probability at least $$1 - \tfrac1N$$ every pair satisfies the inequality above: all squared distances are preserved up to a factor $$1 \pm \varepsilon$$.

**Proof.** For a unit vector $$x$$, $$Qx$$ has independent $$\mathcal{N}(0, \tfrac1n)$$ entries, so the unit-norm lemma applies to $$x = \frac{v_i - v_j}{\lVert v_i - v_j \rVert}$$. A union bound over the $$\binom N2$$ pairs gives a failure probability of at most

$$2\binom{N}{2} e^{-n\varepsilon^2/8} \le N^2 \cdot N^{-3} = \frac1N$$

**The figure.** The experiment uses 30 random points in $$\mathbb{R}^{200}$$ and one Gaussian projection. The histogram shows the 435 ratios of squared distances after and before projection, and the brown band is $$1 \pm \varepsilon$$. Lower the target dimension $$n$$ and the histogram spreads out; raise it and it tightens around 1.

The guarantee is conservative. At $$\varepsilon = 0.5$$ the lemma asks for $$n \ge 327$$, more than the original 200 dimensions, yet far smaller $$n$$ keeps every pair in the band. Note also that $$n$$ depends only on $$N$$ and $$\varepsilon$$, not on $$m$$. That is why random projection is a cheap way to shrink data before nearest-neighbour search or clustering: sampling $$Q$$ costs nothing compared with orthogonalizing a basis.
